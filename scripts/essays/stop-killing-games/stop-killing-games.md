## Real Problem, Bad Solution

Stop Killing Games is a deeply confused movement. To be sure, the movement points at a real problem with the way that the video game industry is operating. But the proposed solution of government regulations can only bring about further problems and make the industry worse for both producers and consumers.

In short, the Stop Killing Games initiative seeks to address the problem of games publishers arbitrarily making games unplayable without any recourse for the consumer at a time of their choosing. The supporters say that games should operate like any other commodity: if I purchase some good at a store, the expectation is that I get to keep it forever, it can&rsquo;t be taken away from me at a later point. Or if I purchase some service, like a hair cut, there is an understanding that it wont be stopped half-way through; there is to be an understood time at which the service is done and we can move on with our days.

To make games operate in this way, the proposal is that big governments like the EU should come in to regulate the games industry. Force game publishers to develop future games with an &ldquo;end of life&rdquo; plan that would leave the game reasonably functional after the publisher no longer wishes to support it; and that these end of life plans should be known at the time of purchase.

I contend that the movement has identified a real problem here, and I am not interested in quibbling over any of the finer points about what counts as reasonably functional or anything like that. Basically I am not attacking this from the Pirate Software viewpoint. My view is that this proposed solution does not get at the heart of the issue and is destined to have unintended consequences. It fails both as a permanent solution and as a half-measure or &ldquo;baby step&rdquo; towards the ideal. This is because the real problem that Stop Killing Games identifies is caused by the fact that in the first place the government grants monopoly protections to these game publishers in the form of copyrights and patents.

## The Anti-IP stance

That so-called &ldquo;intellectual property&rdquo; rights are really at the core of this problem can be seen blatantly in the numerous examples of killed games. Ubisoft Ivory Tower cites as the reason they shut down The Crew: that &ldquo;it has become a necessity due to upcoming server infrastructure and **licensing constraints**.&rdquo;<sup><a id="fnr.1" class="footref" href="#fn.1" role="doc-backlink">1</a></sup> That is, some third party IPs, for instance car brands, are getting in the way of keeping the game going.

Marvel heroes was killed off and the community have since revived it on their own. But Disney could literally decide tomorrow to nuke them with a million lawsuits because Disney holds the copyrights to any Marvel properties.

Spec Ops: The Line was delisted from storefronts due to expiring music licenses.<sup><a id="fnr.2" class="footref" href="#fn.2" role="doc-backlink">2</a></sup>

I could go on, but I think you get the picture. The common thread with all of these examples, and in fact most of the bad business practices that exist, is that none of it would be possible without the monopoly protections afforded by IP. That if I come up with an idea first, I thereby get to prevent anybody else from implementing that idea in their own work.

This is a total anathema to how human progress works. If the primitive man who discovered fire was able to enforce a monopoly over it, we would still be shivering in caves. It is the very fact that upon this discovery, everyone else who saw it was able to freely replicate it&#x2013;that is copy the information&#x2013;that allowed it to spread from tribe to tribe and kick-start the technological development of mankind.

From a legal standpoint, there is really nothing about IP that makes it a genuine form of property. Patent attorney and contemporary jurist, Stephan Kinsella, points this out in his book &ldquo;Against Intellectual Property.&rdquo; The very concept of &ldquo;property&rdquo; comes about due to scarcity, that is if you are there using a stick to spearfish, I can&rsquo;t use that same stick to stoke my fire at the same time. Your action excludes my action and vice versa. It is from this fact that we need property rights to decide who should be allowed to use the stick. To say that you own the stick means that you should be allowed to keep spearfishing and I should not stop you.

Now, none of this applies when it comes to ideas. If you simply come up with a new design of spear that will make it way better to fish with&#x2014;that idea is not scarce. I am able to also use that idea to build my own advanced spear with my own property and it does not diminish your ability to keep using the idea yourself! That is, the concept of &ldquo;property&rdquo; simply does not arise in the realm of ideas, only in the realm of scarce goods.

Really what these IP monopolies are are government-enforced easements over the property of everybody else. They are a piece of legislative fiat that says because you came up with a certain type of idea you now have a partial ownership in the property of everybody else. That you get to tell me what I can do with my own property. That&rsquo;s not right at all. It&rsquo;s my property and all the guns and tanks in the world doesn&rsquo;t give you the right to tell me what I am allowed to do with it.

Writing a piece of paper that says Jewish people no longer have rights certainly didn&rsquo;t give the Germans the right to slaughter them. But that&rsquo;s exactly the argument they gave. They said that under German law it was not a crime to kill them, so accordingly they didn&rsquo;t actually murder anybody. The prosecution at the Nuremberg trials were having none of this. They recognised that a man&rsquo;s rights are inherent in his nature. That no amount of government fiat is able to take that away from him.

I can&rsquo;t go into the full philosophical justification for this thesis, called natural law theory, in this video; but you can find out about that here, or in my free course linked in the description.

The point is that in these examples of killed games, when developer support is revoked there is nothing stopping the community from stepping in and maintaining the game themselves, except for IP. The Project T.A.H.I.T.I. devs are evidently able to maintain the online services of Marvel Heroes, but they can be shut down any day if a Disney lawyer decides to do it. It is a crime according to the US Government&rsquo;s DMCA and similar legislation elsewhere to attempt to circumvent DRM software, or reverse-engineer and make cracked copies of games. Even if those games are no longer for sale. Without IP standing in the way, there is literally nothing stopping interested communities, or even third party companies, from taking up the mantle and maintaining defunct games, or even making forked versions of living games.

Gamers have been the frog boiling in the IP pot for many years now. This isn&rsquo;t just about games being killed without any end-of-life plans. Think of how great it would be if you didn&rsquo;t like some new balance patch for your favourite shooter and instead of having no recourse, you could just spin up your own server with whatever rules you think would be most fun to play with?

Imagine if you had a great idea of a story that could be told in the world of Pokemon, and you could just make a new Pokemon game that the community could either play or not, without fear of Nintendo annihilating you with their orbital laser, or whatever it is they do with modders.

What if you were free to emulate and preserve old games at your will? Run whatever software you want on your own computer? Make any modifications you wish to that software? All without the looming threat of big brother stomping you into the ground?

Wouldn&rsquo;t that be better?

## Regulations Never Work

So not only has the Stop Killing Games initiative missed the mark when it comes to correctly identifying the root cause of the problem; their proposed solution also gets us nowhere closer to a games industry that actually works for the consumer. Regulations like the ones being proposed always end up causing a litany of unforeseen second and third order effects.

Kentucky legislators wished to protect established moving companies, and protect consumers from shady operators. They wanted &ldquo;healthy companies in Kentucky.&rdquo; Their solution was to attack so-called dog-eat-dog competition. Movers in Kentucky were required to obtain a &ldquo;certificate of necessity&rdquo; from the Kentucky Movers Association to prove that there was &ldquo;room in the market&rdquo; for them to operate. This association was, of course, to be headed by experts in the moving market. That being people who already ran moving companies. So the very people who new companies would be competing against were the ones to decide whether they could operate. This monopoly power afforded to a cartel of moving companies leads to worse services for the consumer. Regulations to protect the consumer ended up hurting the consumer.<sup><a id="fnr.3" class="footref" href="#fn.3" role="doc-backlink">3</a></sup>

Regulators have long been concerned with protecting teachers unions. The goal is to ensure that teachers make a &ldquo;living wage.&rdquo; The reality is that despite union protection of teachers and increasing the federal spending on education every year, test scores have not gone up. The unions make it so that teachers lack an incentive to teach well, they can&rsquo;t get fired, and the government schools have an effective monopoly on education, so there is no competition.<sup><a id="fnr.4" class="footref" href="#fn.4" role="doc-backlink">4</a></sup> It is so difficult to fire teachers, in fact, that even an openly abusive teacher wasn&rsquo;t fired for years.<sup><a id="fnr.5" class="footref" href="#fn.5" role="doc-backlink">5</a></sup> Does that strike you as protecting students and good teachers; or protecting bad teachers from getting their just desserts? Again, no matter the intention of the regulation, it leads to these higher-order effects that you can&rsquo;t foresee.

Take the case of mandatory minimum sentences. These are intended to discourage crime and encourage criminals to snitch on their buddies to get a lighter sentence. But this means that if they have nobody to snitch on, they just make things up about innocent people who then have to go to jail for a very long time.<sup><a id="fnr.6" class="footref" href="#fn.6" role="doc-backlink">6</a></sup>

Or how about Title IX; which was intended to prevent sex-based discrimination and ensure gender equality. It forced schools to have proportionate representation in their sports teams, so if a college is 50% female, 50% of the athletes also must be female. If not as many women are interested in sports, male teams get cut. A regulation that was supposed to bring about equality led instead to discrimination against men. Of course, before Title IX was introduced, schools were already adjusting to the market demand and admitting women. If there is a demand, the market will follow it. Today more women than men go into higher education.<sup><a id="fnr.7" class="footref" href="#fn.7" role="doc-backlink">7</a></sup>

I could go on ad infinitum; drug safety laws making drugs prohibitively expensive, leaving the poor unable to afford the medicine in the first place;<sup><a id="fnr.8" class="footref" href="#fn.8" role="doc-backlink">8</a></sup> EPA regulations shutting down a mine, costing the jobs of rural Alaskan workers, out of fear that it would somehow pollute a bay 90 miles away without any environmental impact report as proof;<sup><a id="fnr.9" class="footref" href="#fn.9" role="doc-backlink">9</a></sup> or even how price gouging laws prevent the flow of goods into disaster zones because nobody wants to truck a bunch of generators into a dangerous area if they are only allowed to sell them at cost.<sup><a id="fnr.10" class="footref" href="#fn.10" role="doc-backlink">10</a></sup>

I bring this all up because if this movement is able to push for legislation, then it must also be able to push the abolition of legislation. The initiative as it stands can only serve to make things worse. Making things worse is wrong, even if making things better is hard. Stop Killing Games is not a band-aid, or a baby step in the right direction&#x2014;it is seeking a new, destructive interference in the market by the government, which can only serve to hurt more people and violate more rights. As the greatest ever economist, Ludwig von Mises, once noted: upon seeing some problem caused by a government intervention, there exists a tendency to demand a further intervention to fix it. This regulatory spiral may continue on until all capital is destroyed and man is returned to a hunter-gatherer existence.


<a id="orgca557bd"></a>

## The Good, The Bad, and the Ugly

As far as I see it, there are basically three sides to this issue: the good, the bad, and the ugly.

IP abolition is the correct stance here, it should be the focus. But the bad solution which is more government regulation has received overwhelming support because the one vocal dissenter is an idiot who doesn&rsquo;t even understand the proposal in the first place. I believe that there could be a lot of good people who are swept up in support of Stop Killing Games purely because the alternative presented has been either that, or Pirate Software. These people should recognise that like with previous mass internet movements, its not as simple as getting the government to fix it.

Remember back when Net Neutrality was repealled and everyone on the internet made a big fuss over how soon you would have to pay by the tweet? Notice how despite this universal consensus that the regulation was utterly necessary, the world did not actually collapse from its repeal. You should bare this in mind with the Stop Killing Games movement. Pause for a second, and try to consider whether tossing sawdust will really be able to put out that gasoline fire you see raging around you.
